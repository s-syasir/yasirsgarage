## What it is

A Premier League season projector that simulates the remaining fixtures 10,000 times and outputs
final position probability distributions for every team. Three models, all built from scratch in a
single Jupyter notebook — no black-box ML libraries.

| Layer | Method | What it estimates |
|-------|--------|-------------------|
| Team strength | Elo ratings | Relative quality, updated after every match |
| Goal rates | Dixon-Coles Poisson regression | Expected goals per fixture, fitted on xG |
| Season outcomes | Monte Carlo × 10,000 | Probability distributions over final positions |

## How it works

**Elo ratings** update after each result. The rating change scales with how surprising the result
was — a win against a much stronger team moves the needle more than beating a weaker side. Home
teams get a +100 point bonus built into the expected score formula.

**Dixon-Coles** fits attack and defence parameters for each team using maximum likelihood estimation
on historical xG data. The model includes a low-score correction (the ρ parameter) that accounts for
the non-independence of 0-0 and 1-0 scorelines — standard Poisson underestimates how often these
happen.

**Monte Carlo simulation** uses those fitted parameters to simulate each remaining fixture as a
Poisson draw, accumulates points, and repeats 10,000 times. The output is a full probability matrix:
for each team, the probability of finishing in each position.

## Why I built it

Honestly, I watched money ball and was very curious about the basic models you can use to predict 
whole leagues and games. Right now, there is more data collected about individual games than ever before.
Surely there are interesting ways to parse that data and use it to make future predictions.

I think the 25-26 season is for the Premier League is very interesting to watch. Liverpool was the bookmakers' 
favorite. However, as the season progressed, it became evident that wasn't the case. The basic models that 
I worked with here also predicted Liverpool as the winners, so clearly there are things the models just
can't predict.

The real goal here was to actually understand how prediction models work than plug
data into sklearn and read off numbers. Building Elo from scratch, fitting a Poisson regression by
hand, and wiring up the simulation loop forced me to understand each assumption these models make
and where they all break down.

Also, please watch Moneyball. It's an incredible movie.

## Honest limitations

Elo and Dixon-Coles both assume teams are stationary across a season. In reality a manager sacking,
an injury crisis, or a transfer window changes a team fundamentally. The models don't know that —
they only know the results. Projections get more reliable as the season progresses and there's more
data to fit.
